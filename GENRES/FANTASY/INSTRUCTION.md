# GENRE INSTRUCTION — FANTASY

## Genre identity and promise

Fantasy invites the audience into a world where the extraordinary is real and consequential. The audience should experience wonder, danger, discovery or mythic meaning while understanding the emotional and practical rules of the world.

Fantasy can be high fantasy, low fantasy, dark fantasy, heroic fantasy, sword-and-sorcery, fairy tale, mythic fantasy or a hybrid. The project brief must choose the relevant subgenre and tone; do not assume every fantasy story needs kings, prophecies, dragons or medieval Europe.

## Narrative engine

Build the story around a character desire that collides with forces, rules or history larger than the character. Magic, ancient objects, monsters and kingdoms should create choices and consequences, not solve every problem conveniently.

Establish:
- What the protagonist wants and why it matters personally.
- What blocks that goal and how the opposition adapts.
- What a magical or supernatural element can and cannot do.
- What is at stake if the protagonist fails.
- What the protagonist must choose, sacrifice, learn or change.

## World rules and continuity

- Define the world's rules only to the level needed by the story.
- Magic needs limits, costs, conditions or consequences. Avoid unlimited abilities that dissolve tension.
- Keep geography, travel time, factions, names, technology, history and social rules consistent.
- Reveal lore through action, objects, conflict and character behavior rather than long exposition.
- Distinguish established canon from new proposals and unanswered world-building questions.

## Structure and pacing

Use discovery, escalating tests, reversals and meaningful consequences. A mystery about the world can drive the plot, but the protagonist's choices must drive the episode.

Balance quiet character moments with spectacle. Every major set piece should alter the situation, reveal information or force a decision. Do not add a monster or magical duel merely because the genre permits them.

## Characters and conflict

Give heroes, rivals, mentors, creatures and antagonists understandable motives. Avoid reducing characters to alignment labels. A monster can be frightening and still follow rules; an antagonist can be persuasive without being correct.

Use relationships, competing loyalties, debts, bargains, inheritance, identity, fear and ambition to create personal stakes inside large-scale conflict.

## Dialogue and voice

Keep fantasy terminology understandable from context. Use distinctive voices rather than giving every character archaic speech. Avoid constant faux-medieval wording, exposition disguised as dialogue and interchangeable character voices.

## Visual and sound opportunities

Look for strong silhouettes, readable props, evocative environments, magical cause-and-effect, scale contrast, creature movement and memorable sound motifs. Every effect should support a story beat. Design scenes so that their key action remains readable at the project's chosen animation technique and budget.

## Common failure modes

- Inconsistent magic rules or effortless last-minute solutions.
- Lore dumps that stop the story.
- Generic prophecy or chosen-one plotting without character agency.
- Spectacle without emotional consequence.
- Too many invented names and factions for the runtime.
- Anachronistic dialogue used without a clear stylistic reason.
- Expensive visual demands that do not justify their story value.

## Production implications

Identify reusable locations, hero props, creature designs, costumes, factions, magic effects and recurring visual motifs during breakdown. Reuse assets where continuity allows. Mark unique hero assets separately from background details and one-off effects. Tool choice belongs to the task-based tool catalogue and approved project toolchain, not this genre profile.

## Screenplay review checklist

- [ ] The fantasy promise and subgenre are clear.
- [ ] Magic and world rules are internally consistent.
- [ ] The protagonist makes consequential choices.
- [ ] Lore is revealed through the drama.
- [ ] Set pieces change the story.
- [ ] Character voices are distinct and accessible.
- [ ] Visual demands fit runtime and production constraints.
- [ ] New canon, assumptions and open questions are separated.

## Interaction with secondary genres and channel style

Secondary genres may add comedy, mystery, romance, horror or adventure, but fantasy world rules and the primary promise remain coherent. A channel style guide may alter episode length, narration, editing rhythm, humor density, visual branding and packaging; it must not silently change fantasy canon or magic rules.
