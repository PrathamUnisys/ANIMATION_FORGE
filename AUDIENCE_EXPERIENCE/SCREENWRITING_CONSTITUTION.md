# Screenwriting Constitution — Proposal

> **Status: DRAFT PROPOSAL.** This is advisory design material, not an active global instruction. It does not supersede approved canon, project briefs, genre profiles, rights restrictions, or existing governance.

## 1. Purpose

Define a consistent, model-independent standard for developing and reviewing compelling, coherent, visually producible animated stories. The standard guides creative work; it does not prescribe one formula for every genre, format, or story.

## 2. Authority and precedence

Apply this standard only within the existing authority model:

1. System, security, and application-enforced permissions.
2. Global constitution and governance.
3. Project restrictions and approved brief.
4. Approved series/project canon and accepted creative decisions.
5. Primary and compatible secondary genre profiles.
6. Series format and channel-style guidance in their respective scopes.
7. This creative standard and relevant Audience Experience playbooks.
8. Department, agent, provider, and task instructions.

This proposal cannot override approved canon, a project-specific approved brief, genre rules, rights restrictions, or human approval policy. Genre governs narrative conventions; channel style governs presentation and publishing format. Audience Experience is cross-cutting guidance, not a new authority tier.

## 3. Creative principles

### 3.1 Audience promise
- State the experience the episode intends to deliver in one clear sentence.
- Ensure premise, opening, title/thumbnail proposal, story progression, and ending support the same promise.
- Treat audience and format assumptions as explicit inputs, not facts inferred from a title.
- Do not promise a genre experience the screenplay does not deliver.

### 3.2 Story causality and coherence
- Important events should follow from established conditions, character choices, or clearly introduced rules.
- Establish information and capabilities required for a solution before the solution depends on them.
- Distinguish coincidence used to begin a story from coincidence used to resolve its central conflict.
- Track unresolved questions, contradictions, and assumptions.
- A twist should change interpretation without making earlier scenes dishonest.

### 3.3 Character agency and motivation
- Make the protagonist's objective, stakes, and meaningful choices understandable.
- Decisions should be consistent with established motivation or supported by a legible change.
- Opposition should have a comprehensible motive, even when morally wrong.
- Emotional turns should arise from events and relationships shown in the story.

### 3.4 Hook and promise fulfilment
- The opening should create a relevant question, tension, emotional connection, or striking image.
- The hook must connect to the actual story and receive a meaningful payoff.
- Do not use unrelated shock, false stakes, or artificial interruptions as retention devices.
- No universal hook deadline is mandated; evaluate the opening against format, genre, and story intent.

### 3.5 Clue, mystery, and reveal integrity
- A deduction must be supported by information available to the audience or protagonist at the relevant time.
- Distinguish clue, red herring, inference, reveal, and confirmation.
- Establish supernatural, technological, or magical rules and their limits before they become decisive.
- A reveal should answer the central question and account for important evidence.
- Apply mystery-specific requirements only when the selected genre/story engine makes them relevant.

### 3.6 Scene purpose and progression

Each scene should perform one or more identifiable functions: advance objective, introduce or transform conflict, reveal information, change a relationship, alter emotional state, establish a rule, deliver a payoff, or create a necessary transition. Quiet scenes are valid when they create meaningful change or atmosphere with intent.

### 3.7 Dialogue and visual storytelling
- Dialogue should sound speakable, reflect character voice, and serve action, conflict, relationship, or subtext.
- Do not insert brief wording mechanically into dialogue when a story beat can be conveyed naturally.
- Prefer observable action, image, sound, and performance over explanatory dialogue when they communicate more clearly.
- Visual motifs should develop or change meaning; decorative detail is not a substitute for story information.

### 3.8 Emotional integrity and ending
- Earn emotional responses through setup, choice, consequence, and character relationship.
- Resolve the central story question to the degree promised by the format.
- Leave ambiguity intentionally, not because essential causal information is missing.
- The final beat should express consequence, transformation, thematic meaning, or a deliberate series question.

### 3.9 Production integrity
- Write filmable action and distinguish essential story information from optional visual embellishment.
- Identify story-critical props, locations, characters, visual effects, and audio cues.
- Flag production complexity and unknown requirements instead of assuming a tool or asset exists.
- A screenplay-level audio or visual note does not prove that final media quality has been achieved.

### 3.10 Runtime integrity
- Label runtime values as estimates until measured in an animatic or final render.
- Record the estimation method and assumptions.
- Use the selected format and story needs; do not force a universal scene count, beat interval, or runtime formula.

## 4. Requirement classification

Every requirement imported from an episode brief or task must be classified using REQUIREMENT_CLASSIFICATION.md. Mandatory story beats and clues require their narrative function, not necessarily verbatim wording. Only EXACT_TEXT requires exact reproduction.

## 5. Audience Experience artifacts

Where the workflow supports them, maintain traceable links among:
- Audience Plan and audience promise.
- Hook options and selected opening.
- Packaging options and the promise they represent.
- Outline and scene identifiers.
- Retention Map beats and actual screenplay scene/beat identifiers.
- Creative Review findings and exact evidence references.
- Revision lineage and the source revision reviewed.

Do not claim an artifact is generated, persisted, schema-valid, or linked unless the relevant operation and evidence exist.

## 6. Review method

1. Read the screenplay without companion metadata to test basic comprehension.
2. Check premise, promise, protagonist objective, stakes, and causal spine.
3. Check character motivation, choices, and emotional progression.
4. Check clue/reveal fairness and genre-specific rules where relevant.
5. Check scene purpose, pacing, dialogue, visual storytelling, and ending.
6. Check production feasibility and runtime estimate.
7. Compare any Retention Map and packaging proposal against the actual screenplay.
8. Report evidence, interpretation, severity, recommendation, and unchecked areas separately.

## 7. Severity and approval

Use evidence-backed findings and the repository's established severity vocabulary: BLOCKER, MAJOR, MINOR, NOTE. Severity describes the impact of a finding; it is not a numeric creative score.

Creative critique, deterministic validation, technical QC, and human approval remain separate. No score, average, agent recommendation, or successful technical check can approve a creative deliverable. Only the configured human approval path can do so.

## 8. Non-goals

This standard does not require a three-act structure for every story, a new twist/cut/cliffhanger at fixed intervals, universal retention thresholds, or a fixed average score. It does not guarantee audience retention or commercial performance, replace genre/format/channel-style/rights/production policies, or claim unimplemented capabilities.

## 9. Activation

Activation requires a reviewed and approved change request that identifies exact files, compatibility decisions, tests, rollback procedure, and agent/schema/workflow integration. Until then, this file is advisory design material only.
