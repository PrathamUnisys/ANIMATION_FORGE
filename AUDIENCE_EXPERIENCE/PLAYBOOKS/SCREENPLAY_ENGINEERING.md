# Screenplay Engineering Playbook

**Status:** DRAFT GUIDANCE — documentation only; not an active agent instruction.

A repeatable, evidence-led process for turning a story idea into a clear, emotionally effective, animation-aware screenplay. This is a flexible sequence of creative decisions, not a mandatory formula, fixed act structure, scene count, beat interval, or retention guarantee.

## Operating principles

1. Story before packaging. Titles, thumbnails, hooks, and retention maps must represent the story that actually exists.
2. Specificity before complexity. Prefer one memorable premise with clear consequences over accumulated lore and arbitrary twists.
3. Causality before surprise. Surprising events should still make sense after the reveal.
4. Character choice before plot convenience. Important turns should be driven by decisions, not only coincidence or exposition.
5. Filmable writing. Write what the audience can see and hear; flag what cannot yet be staged.
6. Evidence before revision. Identify the exact weakness and its consequence before changing a scene.
7. Smallest effective change. Preserve strong material; do not rewrite an entire screenplay to fix one local defect.
8. Human authority. Briefs, canon decisions, creative approvals, and consequential production choices remain subject to existing approval policy.

## Stage 0 — Intake and creative brief

Collect or explicitly mark unknown: premise and source; primary and secondary genre; format; intended audience and viewing context; target runtime and aspect ratio; series relationship; approved canon and world rules; required beats, clues, exact text and exclusions; rights/research constraints; production limits; and intended audience emotion or understanding.

**Output:** A concise brief separating confirmed facts, assumptions, open decisions, and source references. Never invent missing canon to make the brief look complete.

**Gate:** Surface conflicts with approved canon or ambiguous requirements for human clarification.

## Stage 1 — Find the dramatic engine

Answer in plain language:
- Who is the story primarily about?
- What do they want right now?
- Why does it matter now?
- What or who resists them?
- What happens if they fail?
- What makes the situation difficult?
- What choice must the protagonist eventually make?
- What changes by the end?

Write a one-sentence premise and a short paragraph explaining the dramatic engine. A mystery may be driven by a question; a comedy by a contradiction; a tragedy by an irreversible choice; an adventure by a goal and escalating obstacles. Do not force every genre into the same engine.

## Stage 2 — Define the audience promise

Write one sentence describing the experience the episode intends to deliver. Identify the central question/tension/relationship, the genre experience, what the viewer should understand by the ending, the emotional residue, and where the opening promise is fulfilled. This is an editorial test, not a marketing slogan.

## Stage 3 — Build character pressure and change

For each major character, define only what the story needs:

| Element | Question |
|---|---|
| Want | What do they actively pursue? |
| Fear / cost | What loss makes the choice difficult? |
| Strategy | How do they try to get what they want? |
| Blind spot / need | What truth or limitation are they avoiding, if relevant? |
| Pressure | What forces their strategy to change? |
| Choice | What consequential decision do they make? |
| Consequence | What changes because of it? |

Do not force every character into a neat arc. Characters may resist change or make tragic choices, but their behaviour must be intelligible from the information provided. Give opposition a motive, objective, and method.

## Stage 4 — Design the story spine

Outline the causal chain: initial condition and disruption; protagonist response and first meaningful choice; obstacles; new information or consequence; escalation; climax decision/action; consequence and emotional resolution.

For every major turn ask: **Because of what just happened, what does the character do next?** If the answer is only "because the plot needs it", repair the causal bridge. This is a diagnostic spine, not a required seven-beat structure.

## Stage 5 — Track rules, clues, setups, and reveals

For mysteries, speculative fiction, fantasy, horror, procedural stories, or any plot depending on special knowledge/rules, maintain a writer-facing logic ledger:

| Item | Record |
|---|---|
| Story question | What is the audience trying to understand? |
| Ground rules | What can and cannot happen? |
| Clue | What observable fact is introduced? |
| First availability | When can the audience notice it? |
| Character knowledge | Who notices or understands it, and when? |
| Initial / true meaning | What does it seem to mean, and what does it mean after the reveal? |
| Red herring | What plausible interpretation misdirects, and why? |
| Payoff | Where is the clue explained or used? |

Run three fairness tests: could an attentive viewer notice decisive evidence in time; does the protagonist have a credible path from evidence to conclusion; and does the solution obey rules established before it becomes decisive?

A clue can be subtle, but should not be invisible, retroactively invented, or withheld solely to fake a twist. Red herrings should be explainable. Reveals should account for important evidence. For non-mystery stories, adapt the ledger to promises, objects, relationships, rules, and emotional setups.

## Stage 6 — Create outline and scene cards

For each scene record: ID/location/time; character objective; conflict/obstacle; what changes; new information, clue, choice, or emotional turn; visual and audio carrier; continuity; production dependencies; and setup/payoff links.

A scene should usually change something meaningful: objective, power, information, relationship, danger, emotion, or audience understanding. Quiet scenes are valid when they create meaningful change or deliberate atmosphere.

**Scene test:** If removed, what breaks, disappears, or becomes weaker? If nothing, consider cutting, combining, or clarifying its function.

## Stage 7 — Design the opening

Generate a few genuinely different openings. For each record the opening image/action/sound, question or emotional connection, what the viewer understands, story connection, exact later fulfilment beat, and any risk of misleading the viewer. Choose the option that best expresses the actual story. A quiet emotional opening can be stronger than spectacle. Avoid false stakes and unrelated shock.

## Stage 8 — Write the screenplay

Write from the approved outline and scene cards:
- Use clear, observable, filmable action.
- Separate action, dialogue, narration, sound, and visual/camera notes.
- Give characters distinct speech shaped by intention and emotional state.
- Let dialogue achieve something, conceal something, avoid something, or change a relationship.
- Use subtext when useful; do not make every line cryptic.
- Avoid dialogue that repeats what the image already communicates.
- Make visual clues legible, then let the audience interpret.
- Keep geography, entrances/exits, object handling, eyelines, and cause/effect stageable.
- Mark optional embellishment separately from story-critical action.
- Distinguish mandatory story beats, mandatory clues, and exact text.

Camera directions should clarify information or emotion, not decorate every line.

## Stage 9 — Build the Retention Map from the script

Create the map from the actual screenplay revision, not the outline. For each meaningful beat record exact scene/beat reference; what the viewer knows before it; active question/expectation; what changes; intended emotional shift; visual/audio carrier; setup/payoff links; and estimated duration only where useful.

Verify every reference against the exact script revision. Mark unresolved references. The map is an editorial model, not measured audience behaviour.

## Stage 10 — Independent review

Review the screenplay without relying on companion metadata first. Then inspect the map and production notes. Assess promise, premise, objective/stakes, character motivation, causality, setup/payoff, clue fairness, scene purpose, dialogue, visual/audio storytelling, emotional credibility, genre, originality, ending, feasibility, and runtime evidence as relevant.

Every finding cites a scene/beat/line, separates observation from interpretation, explains likely consequence, and suggests a targeted action. Mark unchecked areas. Never invent validator results or use an uncalibrated score average as a gate.

## Stage 11 — Revise by priority

- **BLOCKER:** contradiction, missing prerequisite, or logic failure prevents the story functioning.
- **MAJOR:** substantial comprehension, motivation, causal, emotional, or production problem.
- **MINOR:** localized weakness with limited impact.
- **NOTE:** optional taste, polish, or alternative.

Revision order: story logic and causal bridges; motivation/stakes/choices; clue/reveal fairness and rule setup; scene order/progression; dialogue and visual/audio delivery; language polish.

After revision, recheck affected scenes and downstream payoffs. Preserve previous revisions and record what changed, why, and which findings it addresses.

## Stage 12 — Final handoff

Include exact screenplay ID/revision and source brief; logline and audience promise; genre/format; outline and scene list; screenplay; requirement traceability; Retention Map tied to the exact revision; open questions; production-critical cast/sets/props/effects/audio; runtime method and uncertainty; review findings and responses; deterministic QC by individual check including NOT_TESTED; and human approval state separately.

Do not call a draft approved, production-ready, measured, or schema-valid without supporting evidence and the configured approval path.

## Practical use

Complete Stages 0–4 before drafting. Then work through 5–8 as relevant, create the map from the screenplay, and finish with review and revision. Combine stages for simple formats. The goal is not more paperwork; it is a stronger screenplay and a clear reason why it is stronger.
