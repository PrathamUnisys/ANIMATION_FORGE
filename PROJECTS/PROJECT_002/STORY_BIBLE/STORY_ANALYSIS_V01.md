# Story Analysis - The Dragon-Slaying Refund

- **Project ID:** `PROJECT_002`
- **Stage:** `STORY_ANALYSIS`
- **Revision:** 1
- **Status:** Draft - awaiting human review
- **Format:** 10-minute adult fantasy comedy

## Core premise

A furious knight seeks compensation after spending three days travelling to a
dragon-slaying quest site, only to find the dragon already dead. A goblin clerk
tries to apply the guild's rules to the knight's increasingly implausible claim.
The knight's supposed proof is revealed to be a chicken bone, and the claim is
rejected.

## Story engine

The comedy comes from the mismatch between epic heroic expectations and
small-print administrative procedure. The knight treats time, effort, and
indignation as evidence; the clerk needs a qualifying claim and verifiable
proof. Each attempt to make the case should expose another gap between what the
knight thinks the quest promised and what the guild actually guarantees.

The office is the story's anchor. Brief visual flashbacks to the three-day
journey can raise the stakes and contrast the knight's grand retelling with the
mundane reality, without turning the short into a separate adventure.

## Research grounding and creative application

Preliminary historical reading suggests two useful distinctions:

- Craft guilds are commonly described as regulating production, training, and
  quality within specific trades.
- Merchant guilds are described as regulating trade conditions and, in some
  places, exercising civic or municipal influence. Guilds were not one uniform
  institution across medieval Europe.

The sources below are orientation material, not evidence for a universal
medieval customer-refund system. The quest-refund office should therefore be
treated as an intentional fantasy invention rather than presented as
historical fact.

**Creative application:** Give the adventurers' guild a clear charter and a
narrow claim process: it verifies completed jobs and protects the guild's
reputation, but does not automatically reimburse travel or guarantee that a
target will still be alive. The clerk can be scrupulous rather than cruel. This
makes the disagreement legible: the knight believes the guild sold him a
successful dragon hunt; the guild believes it sold him an opportunity to
attempt one.

To keep the middle active, let each form or piece of evidence expose a
different mismatch between those expectations. Use only a few escalating
checks; don't turn the short into a parade of paperwork jokes. The chicken
bone is the final, decisive proof problem, not an early gag.

**Research sources (accessed 2026-11-09):**

- [Craft guilds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Craft_guilds) - overview of
  trade-specific skill, production, and quality functions; cites Britannica
  and historical scholarship.
- [Merchant guilds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merchant_guilds) - overview

- [Guilds in Medieval Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guilds_in_Medieval_Europe)

## YouTube hook and format assessment

**The hook is the contradiction in the title:** a knight wants a refund because
the dragon was already dead. That is easy to understand, specific to this
fantasy-comedy premise, and promises an argument with a punchline. The chicken
bone is the payoff, not the opening hook.

The earlier outline spent too long arriving at that premise. Open in the
argument, within the first few seconds:

> **KNIGHT:** I want a refund. The dragon was already dead when I got there.  
> **CLERK:** Did you kill the dragon?  
> **KNIGHT:** No.  
> **CLERK:** Then why are you requesting payment?  
> **KNIGHT:** Because I travelled three days to find it dead!

Then move directly into the clerk checking the quest terms. This puts the
title's promise on screen immediately without revealing the chicken-bone gag.
Avoid replaying this exchange later; the story should escalate from it.

**Could it work on YouTube?** The premise has promising ingredients: a clear
title, a relatable frustration, a compact visual setup, and a distinct final
reveal. That is not enough to predict views or guarantee a successful
10-minute video. The main risk is repetition: a single refund argument can
feel stretched unless each new form, memory, or piece of evidence changes the
claim and raises the stakes.

Before committing to a full animated short, test a timed table read or rough
animatic. Check whether viewers understand the premise immediately, whether
the middle keeps changing the situation, and whether the final reveal earns
the wait. A strong thumbnail could contrast the armoured knight and goblin
clerk across the counter, with an oversized mystery bone; do not identify it
as a chicken bone in advance.

YouTube's [audience-retention guidance](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415?hl=en)
specifically calls out the first 30 seconds, matching the title/thumbnail
promise, and experimenting with the intro. Its
[thumbnail and title guidance](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12340300?hl=en)
recommends accurate, succinct packaging and evaluating click-through rate
alongside the video's audience data. Those are useful checks after publishing,
not a way to promise performance in advance.

## Character functions

- **The knight:** Exhausted, proud, and genuinely aggrieved by wasted effort.
  His confidence in the claim grows as its evidence weakens. Play the grievance
  sincerely so the absurdity lands without making him a fool from the outset.
- **The goblin clerk:** Calm, exact, and constrained by procedure. The clerk is
  not a villain; the deadpan questions and routine stamp make the system funny.
- **The next customer:** A closing visual or audio cue that shows the guild
  moving on without ceremony. No additional speaking role is required.

## Proposed 10-minute beat structure

1. **0:00-0:30 - Cold open on the claim.** Play the exchange above in the first
   few seconds. The knight's armour, dusty travel gear, and fury make the
   three-day cost visible. No logo or world-history preamble.
2. **0:30-1:30 - The form defines the dispute.** The clerk checks the quest
   terms. The knight thinks he bought a successful dragon hunt; the guild's
   wording covers an attempt, not a guaranteed live target or travel refund.
3. **1:30-3:00 - Make the wasted trip tangible.** The knight gives a concise,
   heightened account of the road and finding the dragon already dead. Keep
   the images funny and economical; leave the killer unknown.
4. **3:00-4:30 - Look for a category that fits.** The clerk and knight test
   whether the claim is for a failed quest, travel costs, or a completed
   slaying. Each category reveals a new mismatch in their expectations.
5. **4:30-6:30 - The claim escalates.** The knight offers increasingly
   confident but inadequate evidence. Each attempt should change the clerk's
   assessment, not repeat the same objection.
6. **6:30-8:15 - The supposed dragon tooth.** The knight places the gigantic
   object on the counter. The clerk measures or checks it against the guild's
   reference guide. Hold on the knight's confidence as doubt grows.
7. **8:15-9:00 - The finding.** The clerk identifies a chicken bone. After the
   silence, the knight says, "It was a very large chicken."
8. **9:00-10:00 - Rejection and reset.** The clerk stamps `QUEST REJECTED`.
   The knight angrily leaves; the clerk calls the next customer without
   missing a beat.

## Creative strengths

- A clear, filmable central conflict with only two essential characters and
  one main location.
- A repeatable comedic pattern: the knight escalates his argument while the
  clerk reduces it to a form field or rule.
- The chicken-bone reveal is a strong, visual punchline that reinterprets the
  knight's confidence. Keeping it near the end protects its impact.
- The contrast between heavy armour and ordinary office furniture offers
  visual comedy without requiring large-scale dragon animation.
- The central contradiction can be communicated before the title card,
  reducing the chance that viewers mistake the video for generic fantasy
  exposition.

## Risks to manage in screenplay development

- The supplied exchange is a short sketch. The middle must add escalation,
  not simply repeat the same refund argument for ten minutes.
- The 10-minute runtime is not validated yet. If the middle cannot sustain
  distinct turns, shorten the film rather than pad it with repeated form jokes.
- Keep the clerk sympathetic and the rules legible; otherwise the scene may
  read as arbitrary rather than satirical.
- Preserve the chicken-bone reveal. Earlier clues should create suspicion but
  not give away the answer.
- The quest's payment and refund terms are currently ambiguous. Choose whether
  the knight is a guild member, a hired contractor, or a customer before
  scripting the fine print; retain the supplied "refund" premise and dialogue.
- The three-day journey should have a few distinct visual callbacks so its
  cost feels specific without taking too much time away from the office scene.

## Assumptions for review

- The guild office is the primary setting; travel is shown only in brief
  flashbacks.
- The dragon's killer remains unknown and is not required to resolve the
  refund dispute.
- The ending and the chicken-bone punchline remain as supplied.
- The tone is dry, adult-facing fantasy comedy; "adult" does not by itself
  imply graphic or explicit material.
