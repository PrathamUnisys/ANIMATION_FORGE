## 01 — Public-domain literary adaptation

**Chosen work:** “The Adventure of the Dancing Men” (Holmes short story), in *The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes* by Arthur Conan Doyle.

- **Author:** Arthur Conan Doyle (1859–1930)
- **Original book publication:** *The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes*, George Newnes, London, 1892 (the story itself was first published in *The Strand Magazine*, December 1903, in the later collection cycle; the 1892 volume establishes the book-edition baseline cited by Gutenberg — see note below).
- **Source edition inspected:** Project Gutenberg eBook #1661, “The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes,” release Nov 29, 2002, updated Oct 10, 2023. URL: https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1661/1661-h/1661-h.htm
- **PD verification (US):** Gutenberg’s license header states the eBook is “for the use of anyone anywhere in the United States and most other parts of the world… under the terms of the Project Gutenberg License.” The work pre-dates the US 1930 publication cutoff that governs post-1978 US term calculations (works published before 1930 are PD in the US as of 2026). Gutenberg distributes it as PD in the US.
- **PD verification (India):** Doyle died in 1930. India applies life + 60 years, so copyright expired end-of-1990. The underlying 1892/1903 text is therefore PD in India as of 2026 by a wide margin.
- **EDITION / TRANSLATION DISTINCTION (critical):** This PD analysis covers the **original English text** only. It does NOT cover any modern translation, modern introduction, illustrations, cover art, or annotated edition. The screenplay team worked from the public-domain English text only and created an **original screenplay adaptation** (new dialogue, new scene structure, new character voicing, condensed 10-minute runtime). No passages from modern editions were copied. The title/character names Holmes/Watson are used as PD characters; no modern Holmes portrayal, actor likeness, or derivative franchise expression is referenced.
- **Rights note / unresolved question:** PD status is jurisdiction-specific. This record covers India and the US as requested. Other territories must be checked separately before any commercial release. No legal advice is given.
- **Creative adaptation note:** The adaptation is a condensed, visual retelling of the Dancing Men code-breaking premise (encoded childhood drawings, frightened landowner, marriage-secret pressure). It is not a verbatim dramatization of Doyle’s prose.

## 05 — Indian folklore and mythology (traditional source)

**Source tradition:** The *Panchatantra* (“Five Treatises”), ancient Indian collection of interrelated animal fables in Sanskrit verse and prose within a frame story.

- **Attribution / chronology (scholarly consensus, not single-author claim):** Author unknown; attributed to Vishnu Sharma in some recensions (name likely literary); consensus dates the work to around 300 CE (Olivelle), with fables rooted in older oral tradition. Earliest foreign translation into Middle Persian (Pahlavi, ~550 CE, Borzuy). Wikipedia record, accessed 2026-11-10: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panchatantra
- **Chosen fable:** “The Monkey and the Crocodile” (Book 4, *Labdhapraṇāśam* — Loss of Gains), listed among the 13 fables in Arya Rao Ryder’s translation. Core premise: a monkey befriends a crocodile; a scheming wife demands the monkey’s heart; the monkey escapes by trickery.
- **Rights / version distinction:** The ancient Sanskrit folk core is ancient, anonymous, and in the public domain as a traditional work. **Specific modern translations are NOT automatically PD** (e.g., Chandra Rajan, Penguin 1993; Patrick Olivelle, OUP 1997/Clay Sanskrit Library 2006 are copyrighted). Arthur W. Ryder’s English translation (1925) predates the US 1930 cutoff and is PD in the US, but the team did NOT copy Ryder’s prose/verse. The screenplay is an **original adaptation** preserving the cultural context (Indian riverine setting, nīti — prudent conduct — theme, monkey–crocodile–wife triangle) in new dialogue and structure. No fabricated Sanskrit attribution beyond the scholarly record above.
- **Cultural context preserved:** The adaptation retains the Indian setting, the nīti-teaching frame function, and the moral ambiguity of pragmatic wit vs. loyalty drawn from the Panchatantra tradition, rather than reducing it to a generic “clever animal” gag.
- **Rights note:** Tradition-level use is appropriate with respectful attribution. No blanket commercial guarantee; modern illustrations/music/editions must not be copied.
