## Verdict

**Overall: Compelling, understandable explanation with responsible limits, but the trauma-memory passage needs a verifiable source for its peer-reviewed-review attribution.** The script distinguishes the Loftus–Palmer wording experiment from debated reconsolidation theory, and it avoids declaring individual memories true or false. The illustrative opening and final callback form a clean narrative loop. The package is mostly accurate, though the thumbnail may suggest a real disputed memory rather than a clearly hypothetical example.

## Review

### Title and thumbnail

**“How Questions Change What People Remember”** is accessible and click-clear. The Loftus–Palmer experiment supports the core finding that question wording can affect later estimates and reports. However, the title’s broad phrasing can imply a demonstrated change to the stored memory itself; much of the script carefully describes effects on memory reports. A title that makes “reports” or “recall” explicit would track the evidence more narrowly.

The thumbnail's **“A MEMORY OR A HINT?”** is readable and connected to the red-car example. A family photo can suggest a real personal or trauma-memory dispute, while the opening is a hypothetical. The visual label in the script helps, but consider making the illustrative nature clear in the package itself.

### First 30 seconds and retention

At **00:00**, the class-photo scenario gives an immediate, relatable puzzle. The line “That does not prove the car was there” protects against presenting the hypothetical as a real memory test. The central question is clear before the end of the opening section, and the return to the translucent car at the end gives the story a satisfying visual loop.

The progression is logical: ordinary recall (**00:55**), a specific study (**02:25**), how later information can enter a report (**04:15**), limits and sensitive applications (**05:55**), then practical caution (**07:45**). The change from the vivid crash experiment to the limits section gives the middle a useful shift in stakes. The explanation of the misinformation effect is simple and not overburdened with jargon.

### Narration and accessibility

The narration is conversational and clear for a general audience. The script explains its terms and cautions against the simplistic conclusion that “memory is fake.” The opening's “You remember the day your class photo was taken” assumes the viewer has such a memory; this is a common, low-risk conversational device, but could be framed as “Think of a class photo” for wider access.

### Evidence and credibility

The Loftus–Palmer study is described with its essential design: crash footage, different verbs, speed estimates, and a later broken-glass question. The script correctly distinguishes the observed responses from broader claims about all witnesses. Its account of human reconsolidation is also properly marked as contested rather than established fact; the cited 2018 review supports describing the human evidence as mixed and methodologically debated.

**MAJOR — Untraceable trauma-memory attribution, 05:55:** The script says, “A peer-reviewed review of research on recovered memories says they are not automatically true or false; it also notes that the available reports often lack independent proof of what happened.” The source list does not identify that peer-reviewed review. The listed Crown Prosecution Service toolkit is prosecution guidance, not a peer-reviewed review, and the listed Elsey and Chan papers address reconsolidation, not recovered-memory evidence. Because this passage concerns trauma and individual accounts, name and cite the exact peer-reviewed source or revise the attribution to match a source that is actually listed. Do not leave the claim as a citation-free appeal to a review.

**MINOR — Interview-practice sourcing, 04:15:** The recommendations to use neutral questions, preserve the first account, and prevent witnesses from sharing details are presented as practical guidance without a source in the document. Add a suitable source or keep them clearly framed as general investigative safeguards rather than as findings of the Loftus–Palmer experiment.

### Production feasibility

The class-photo build, two versions of a crash clip, overlapping information streams, and simple evidence columns are feasible with modest animation. The instruction not to reenact a real criminal case without separate sourcing is appropriate. The trauma section should use restrained visuals so the video does not imply that a particular person's memory is under examination.
