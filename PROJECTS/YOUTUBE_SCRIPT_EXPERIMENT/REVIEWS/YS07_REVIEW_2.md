# YS07 Revised — Independent Second-Pass Review

**Review type:** Genuinely independent second review, separate from author self-review. I evaluated the current revised package and full script without consulting the original first-review file or change report. This is an editorial assessment, not audience data or approval.

## Overall verdict

**Thoughtful, accessible philosophical script with a strong visual premise and a clear, appropriately open ending.** It makes a difficult identity question understandable without pretending philosophy has settled it. Its main clarity risk is a small ambiguity in the opening about who is present in the room and whether the original person is still there.

## Packaging and opening

The primary title, **“If Two Copies of You Woke Up at Once,”** accurately promises the scenario. The thumbnail's two lit doorways and `WHICH ONE IS YOU?` make the choice legible at a glance. The alternate “Which One Is the Real You?” is punchier but may imply the script will identify a correct copy; the primary title is a better match for the exploratory answer.

At **00:00**, “You wake up in a room with two people who look exactly like you” creates an immediate question, then labels the premise a thought experiment and explicitly says it is not a technology prediction. This establishes the promise and prevents science-fiction packaging from being mistaken for current science.

- **MINOR — Scenario orientation, 00:00 and 04:20:** The opening asks “Which one would you call yourself?” while describing the viewer waking in a room with two other people. Later, the scanner makes two copies but does not state whether the original remains. The diagram of one silhouette splitting into two helps, but one short sentence should establish the viewer's role: for example, that the two figures are possible continuers of the person who entered the scanner. The central puzzle is still understandable, but this would make its setup logically cleaner.

## Story progression and narration

The script builds in clear stages: body versus mind (**01:10**), one copied person (**02:40**), branching copies (**04:20**), then the distinction between identity and what we care about (**06:20**). The ordinary sleep example helps establish continuity before the scanner thought experiment. The Parfit example of a promise both copies can keep gives the abstract question an emotional and practical consequence.

Language is generally plain and listenable. “Personal identity” is defined in simple terms, while “branching” is explained by the visual and the example. The script does not bury the viewer in competing theories. Its repeated reminders that the scanner is fictional and philosophers disagree are useful guardrails, though the disclaimers do not overwhelm the story.

Production is feasible: two matching figures, a branching path, a small set of labeled concepts, and a final two-doorway image can be made with simple animation. The editor notes appropriately avoid presenting copying as established science.

## Credibility and conceptual accuracy

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy supports the script's distinction between bodily/biological and psychological-continuity views, describes fission or branching puzzles, and discusses Parfit's view that psychological connectedness may matter even when strict identity does not. The script presents these as debated philosophical positions, not factual claims about technology or settled answers.
