# YS08 Revised — Independent Second-Pass Review

**Review type:** Genuinely independent second review, separate from author self-review. I evaluated the current revised package and full script without consulting the original first-review file or change report. This is an editorial assessment, not audience data or approval.

## Overall verdict

**Strong, careful history-and-climate script with a well-matched package and an accessible explanation of regional effects.** It avoids turning “Year Without a Summer” into a literal global claim, connects climate to food costs through concrete examples, and gives the ending a clear human consequence. I found no major unresolved credibility or structure issue in the current version.

## Packaging and opening

The title **“The Summer That Never Came”** is vivid and potentially broad, but the opening immediately explains that the phrase is not literal and that impacts varied by region. The thumbnail's snow-covered summer field, route from Indonesia, and `JUNE. SNOW.` text offer a clear, accurate curiosity gap. The opening at **00:00** begins with documented June snow in parts of New England and frost damage, then asks how an eruption thousands of miles away could affect weather there. It establishes the title promise within the first 30 seconds.

## Progression, narration, and accessibility

The story moves from the eruption (**01:15**), to atmospheric particles (**02:50**), to crops and prices (**04:40**), then to the limits of the explanation (**06:30**) and a short final image (**08:00**). Each section answers a question introduced earlier. The lamp-veil analogy makes the sunlight mechanism understandable, while the script's weather-versus-climate definition helps separate daily conditions from long-term patterns.

The regional map and repeated caveats are especially useful: the script says “parts of” New England, Europe, and the western Mediterranean, and explicitly rejects one uniform global winter. Vocabulary is mostly familiar; “sulfate particles” is explained in context. The visuals are feasible for modest production: maps, an atmosphere cutaway, regional shading, crops, and a market/horse sequence. The instruction to avoid a global famine map is sound.

**NOTE — First-listen density, 02:50:** This section has the highest information load: aerosol formation, sunlight, weather/climate, a 2–4°C regional comparison, and different eastern European conditions. The visual treatment should give each idea a brief beat; no rewrite is necessary if the map and captions are paced clearly.

## Evidence and credibility

The claims checked against the cited sources are well bounded. UCAR supports the Tambora link, high-altitude particles, cooling, crop and food-price effects, including the oat/horse transport connection. The National Park Service documents the specific New England examples used at **04:40**: May crop-killing frost, June snow in Albany and Dennysville, and five consecutive frost nights in Cape May. Luterbacher and Pfister support the **02:50** regional 2–4°C comparison against the 1951–1980 reference period, the east-west differences, sulfur dioxide forming sulfate aerosols, and the possibility that stratospheric particles persist for up to a couple of years. The script describes the eruption as a contributing cause and keeps local circulation, trade, and food access in view.
