# YouTube Script Experiment — 10 Script Report

**Status:** Editorial experiment complete; no videos published or audience-tested.  
**Review status:** Ten originals and ten revisions received independent creative review.  
**Runtime basis:** Every final duration is `SCRIPT_ESTIMATE`; none is measured from a recording, animatic, or final video.  
**Approval:** This report does not mark any script approved or production-ready.

## 1. Research method and evidence limits

The research used three kinds of evidence:

1. **Platform guidance.** YouTube’s official materials cover title/thumbnail accuracy, the relationship between appeal, engagement, and satisfaction, title/thumbnail experiments, and video-level retention reports. They describe what to measure and how to interpret signals; they do not establish a universal writing formula. The official retention report defines its intro measure at 30 seconds and warns that spikes may reflect either interest or confusion. See [YouTube title and thumbnail tips](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12340300?hl=en), [YouTube content performance](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/16559650?hl=en), [title/thumbnail tests](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/16391400?hl=en), and [retention key moments](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415?hl=en).
2. **Published creator examples and interviews.** Examples from LEMMiNO, Johnny Harris, Veritasium, Fall of Civilizations, Kurzgesagt, and The Infographics Show were inspected for visible choices such as title framing, chapter progression, questions, and examples. These observations are craft prompts, not evidence that a technique caused views or retention. Creator-process material is self-reported. The full list and limits are in [Research Notes](../RESEARCH/RESEARCH_NOTES.md).
3. **Topic research.** Each script includes source notes; the reviewers checked claims against the linked authoritative, primary, or peer-reviewed sources where available. Disputed interpretations and geographic or scientific limits are called out in the scripts and reviews.

The public examples were not treated as performance datasets. No private retention graphs, controlled experiments, or reliable causal comparisons were available. All conclusions below are editorial judgments about these ten drafts. Actual viewer response remains unknown.

## 5. Cross-script comparison

### Packaging

The clearest packages state one concrete puzzle: the five missile warning (YS02), the undersea cable cut (YS09), and the traffic stop on an apparently clear road (YS10). This is an editorial clarity judgment, not a click prediction. The YS03 “stone” image is visually striking but required careful wording to avoid repeating a false literal claim. The revised YS09 and YS10 titles make the promised question more legible without claiming a universal outcome.

### Openings

The strongest openings begin with the specific event or contradiction already present in the title: London’s air changes, five warnings appear, a bridge twists, or traffic clears without a visible crash. The revisions generally got to that subject earlier. The thought experiment (YS07) needed an extra orientation sentence because an abstract scenario can leave the viewer unsure where they stand. These are observations from this portfolio; no hook type is shown to outperform another.

### Retention and story progression

The most useful progression in these drafts is a chain where each reveal changes the viewer’s explanation: weather → pollution → policy (YS01); satellite warning → human assessment → limits of the counterfactual (YS02); cable → real outage → incomplete redundancy (YS09); small slowdown → controlled experiment → limits of generalization (YS10). The survival and failure stories use changing options and consequences rather than arbitrary countdowns. Several reviews caught repeated explanations; revisions removed them. The portfolio does not support a universal interval for adding a hook, reveal, or pattern interrupt.

### Narration and accessibility

Concrete comparisons help with difficult ideas when they do not replace the evidence: the lamp veil in YS08, route/bridge comparison in YS09, and stadium wave in YS10. The scripts use a general-audience language target, with mature subjects retained. Review findings most often concerned claim scope, orientation, or repeated explanation, rather than vocabulary alone. A read-aloud pass and an “average 12-year-old can follow the sentence” check are useful editorial prompts, but were not validated through audience testing here.

### Endings

The stronger endings return to the opening image and answer the question at the level the evidence allows: a cable can fail without ending service everywhere; a traffic wave can move backward; the Tambora effect was regional, not a uniform missing summer. YS07 leaves the answer philosophically open because the script has established disagreement. The final line should complete or deliberately bound the promise, not force a moral.

### Production feasibility

Maps, simple diagrams, animated routes, and repeated visual motifs make YS03, YS04, YS07, YS09, and YS10 feasible for a solo animator. YS01, YS02, YS05, YS06, and YS08 need more historical or scientific context, but their key turns can still be represented with maps, timelines, silhouettes, and diagrams rather than expensive reenactments. Rights and licenses for archival imagery, music, and third-party footage still need checking. Production feasibility is a desk estimate, not a costed schedule.

## 6. Repeated observations, one-offs, and hypotheses

**Repeated editorial observations in this sample:**

- A clear title/opening/payoff relationship makes the intended story easier to explain and review.
- Concrete examples help when the narration moves into technical or abstract material.
- Reviewers repeatedly found overstatement, repetition, and missing causal context more damaging than the absence of frequent “hooks.”
- A script’s retention map and runtime notes need to match the exact current draft.

**One-off choices worth testing, not standardizing:**

- A philosophical thought experiment can carry emotional stakes through an ordinary promise (YS07).
- A real outage can turn a broad infrastructure explainer into a concrete case (YS09).
- A controlled experiment can serve as the midpoint reveal for an everyday puzzle (YS10).

**Hypotheses for audience testing:**

- Specific contradiction titles may communicate a clearer click promise than broad topic titles.
- A concrete reveal early in the middle may help an explainer maintain momentum.
- A second plain-language analogy may clarify a technical mechanism, but too many analogies may slow it down.
- The first 30 seconds may work better when they establish both the unusual event and the title’s question quickly.

None of these hypotheses is a measured result from this project.

## 7. Common writing failures caught

- A title could imply a result broader than the script can support.
- The viewer’s role in a hypothetical scenario was unclear.
- A technically accurate fact could still be attributed to the wrong paper or source.
- A cause/effect explanation could be repeated after the viewer already understood it.
- A script could imply that one individual caused a system-wide pattern.
- A revision could leave its title, runtime metadata, or Retention Map out of sync with the text.
- An estimate could be reported without explicit speaking-rate and visual-hold assumptions.

## 8. What we learned and what remains unknown

We learned which passages needed revision under evidence-led editorial scrutiny, and which production directions could carry the explanation without expensive scenes. We also found that different subjects benefit from different story engines; these ten should not be forced into one fixed structure.

We do **not** know which title earns more clicks, which opening holds more viewers, whether a visual analogy improves comprehension, or which script creates the strongest satisfaction. We have no published-video analytics, viewer interviews, comprehension tests, or measured runtimes. Predicted retention and editorial confidence are not audience behavior.

## 9. Recommended real-world test

1. Select two scripts that can be produced with similar effort and appeal to overlapping audiences.
2. Record the narration and make animatics; replace all `SCRIPT_ESTIMATE` values with measurements only after timing them.
3. Test materially different, truthful title/thumbnail packages with YouTube’s available experiment tools when eligible. Record dates, traffic context, versions, and the platform’s result; do not treat a title CTR alone as the winner if watch-time share is the tool’s decision measure.
4. After publication, inspect the video-level 30-second intro, retention dips/spikes, average view duration, and satisfaction signals. Treat a spike as a clue to inspect, not proof of why viewers stayed or rewatched.
5. Compare only against similar-length videos and the channel’s own baseline where possible. Review qualitative comments and comprehension issues separately from retention.
6. Revise one major variable at a time where practical and preserve the exact script, package, edit, and analytics snapshot for each version.

This is a test plan, not a guarantee of statistical certainty or channel growth.
