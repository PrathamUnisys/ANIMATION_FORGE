## Script

### 00:00 — Five warnings

**[NARRATION]**  
On 26 September 1983, a Soviet screen reported five American missiles. Stanislav Petrov had to decide whether the warning made sense before anyone knew what had caused it.

The story is often told as one man stopping a nuclear war. That may overstate what we can know. We cannot replay the decisions that would have followed a different report. But Petrov was the duty officer when a new warning system said an attack was underway, and he judged the warning false.

To understand why, we need to see what appeared on his screen, what the system could actually observe, and why the world around that room made every signal feel dangerous.

**[VISUAL]** A map of the Soviet Union at night. Cut to a simplified control panel. Five warning lights appear one by one. Do not show a countdown timer.  
**[SOUND]** A low equipment hum; each light gets a restrained electronic tone.

### 00:55 — What the system watched

**[NARRATION]**  
Petrov was on duty at a command center for the Soviet Oko early-warning satellite system. “Oko” means “eye.” Its job was to watch for signs that the United States had launched nuclear missiles.

Satellites could look toward missile fields from above. If a launch looked bright against the dark Earth, the system could send a warning. That warning was important, but it was still an interpretation of sensor data. The machines could report what they detected. People had to decide what the report meant.

That distinction would become the whole story.

Petrov was not a random visitor who wandered into the room. He was a military officer with experience in the warning system. The National Park Service says he was the duty officer when the system reported a launch from the United States, followed by reports of four more.

The screen showed a small attack. Five missiles were frightening. They were also a strange way to begin the kind of full-scale strike Soviet leaders feared. That mismatch gave Petrov a reason to question the warning, but it did not prove the warning was false.

**[VISUAL]** Diagram of a satellite looking down at Earth. Show a sensor signal becoming a label on a console, then a person examining the label. A small group of five icons appears beside a much larger set of possible targets, without asserting a specific doctrine.  
**[ON-SCREEN TEXT]** `SENSOR READING ≠ CONFIRMED ATTACK`  
**[EDITOR NOTE]** Keep the diagram simple. The distinction is more important than technical detail.

### 02:35 — A tense moment

**[NARRATION]**  
The warning arrived during a period of deep distrust between Washington and Moscow. Just weeks earlier, the Soviet Union had shot down Korean Air Lines Flight 007 after the civilian passenger plane entered Soviet airspace. The incident had made relations even worse.

Petrov later described the atmosphere as tense. In that setting, an alarm could not be treated as a harmless computer glitch. If the warning was real, minutes mattered. If it was wrong, acting on it could help turn fear into a disaster.

The system was new and had limits. The warning came from satellites. Other forms of early warning mattered too, including radar. Petrov questioned why a real attack would appear as a small number of launches and why the warning lacked the kind of confirmation he expected.

Those were clues, not guarantees. A person under pressure rarely gets a neat list marked “true” or “false.” He gets fragments that point in different directions, with too little time to be comfortable.

**[VISUAL]** Timeline: Flight 007, 1 September 1983; false alarm, 26 September 1983. Show the two events as context, not as direct proof of Petrov’s thoughts. Then split the screen into “satellite warning” and “other evidence still needed.”  
**[SOUND]** Radio static briefly enters under “fragments,” then clears.

### 04:20 — The decision

**[NARRATION]**  
Petrov reported that he believed the warning was false. That is the decision we can document. The popular version often adds a scene in which he alone had the power to launch or stop a nuclear response. The evidence does not support that simple picture.

He was part of a chain of command. His job was to assess and report the warning. Soviet leaders and other officers would have had roles in any further decision. Nuclear systems involve procedures, people, and other sources of information. A false warning could still be dangerous, but one officer’s report was not the same thing as a launch button.

So why did his judgment matter? Because early reports shape what people believe is happening. Calling the alarm false could slow the rush toward the worst interpretation. Calling it a confirmed attack could push the system in the other direction. Petrov’s report was one human judgment inside a larger system, and it helped prevent this warning from being treated as certainty.

That is serious enough. We do not need to turn him into a lone superhero.

**[VISUAL]** The console warning moves through a chain of desks and phones. At Petrov’s station, the label changes from `LAUNCH DETECTED` to `ASSESSMENT: FALSE ALARM`. Keep the chain visible; do not depict him controlling weapons directly.  
**[EDITOR NOTE]** Pause briefly after “That is the decision we can document.”

### 06:00 — What caused the warning?

**[NARRATION]**  
Later investigation traced the false warning to the way sunlight reflected off clouds. From the satellite’s view, the light could resemble the bright signal expected from a missile launch.

The system had mistaken a rare combination of sunlight, clouds, and viewing angle for a nuclear attack. The sky had produced a signal the machine was built to fear.

This explanation came after the event. Petrov did not have the final answer while the warning lights were on. He had to judge the evidence he had, not the explanation investigators would find later.

That difference matters whenever we tell a story about a good decision. If we use facts discovered afterward as if the person knew them at the time, we make the choice look easier than it was.

**[VISUAL]** A simple geometry animation: satellite, sun, curved cloud tops. A reflected patch reaches the sensor and triggers the warning label. Use a caption: `Later investigation found sunlight reflected from clouds had confused the system.`  
**[SOUND]** The warning tone resolves into ordinary room hum.

### 07:10 — What we can say

**[NARRATION]**  
Petrov’s decision did not erase the danger of nuclear systems. The false alarm revealed a weakness in a system meant to help leaders make decisions under extreme pressure. It also showed how much depends on people questioning information that looks official and urgent.

Did Petrov save the world? We cannot prove what would have happened if he had reported the warning differently. The National Park Service credits his judgment with preventing an erroneous retaliatory attack. Historians and analysts also point out that a warning passed up the chain would not automatically have produced a launch. Both facts belong in the story.

The careful version is less like a movie. A duty officer saw a warning, noticed reasons to doubt it, and reported his assessment during a dangerous moment. The system was later found to have mistaken reflected sunlight for a launch signal. The outcome of the alternative history remains unknown.

The lesson is not that one calm person can always stop a crisis. It is that a warning system is only as reliable as its evidence, its procedures, and the people willing to question it.

**[VISUAL]** Return to the five lights. One by one they turn off, leaving the reflection diagram in the dark. Then show the same control room as part of a larger chain of people and systems.  
**[SOUND]** No triumphant music. Let the final line sit over a quiet hum.

### 08:35 — Closing image

**[NARRATION]**  
The screen was wrong. Petrov did not know why. He had to make a judgment anyway.

That is what makes the night of 26 September 1983 worth remembering: not a guaranteed story of one man saving the planet, but a real moment when a person treated an alarming signal as evidence to examine, rather than truth to obey.

**[VISUAL]** A satellite passes over a cloud bank. The reflection fades as the camera pulls back to show Earth at night. Fade to black.  
**[SOUND]** Hum ends; one second of silence.
