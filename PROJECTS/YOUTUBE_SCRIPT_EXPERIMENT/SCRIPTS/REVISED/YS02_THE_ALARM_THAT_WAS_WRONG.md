## Script

### 00:00 — Five warnings

**[NARRATION]**  
On 26 September 1983, a Soviet screen reported five American missiles. Stanislav Petrov had to decide whether the warning made sense before anyone knew what had caused it.

Petrov judged the warning false. People later called him “the man who saved the world,” but we cannot know exactly what would have happened if he had reported it differently. First, look at what the system showed him.

**[VISUAL]** A map of the Soviet Union at night. Cut to a simplified control panel. Five warning lights appear one by one. Do not show a countdown timer.  
**[SOUND]** A low equipment hum; each light gets a restrained electronic tone.

### 00:40 — What the system watched

**[NARRATION]**  
Petrov was on duty at a command center for the Soviet Oko early-warning satellite system. “Oko” means “eye.” Its job was to watch for signs that the United States had launched nuclear missiles.

Satellites watched missile fields from above. The system read bright signals as possible launches and sent a warning. That warning mattered, but it was still a machine’s reading of light. People had to decide what the reading meant.

That distinction would become the whole story.

Petrov was not a random visitor who wandered into the room. He was a military officer with experience in the warning system. The National Park Service says he was the duty officer when the system reported a launch from the United States, followed by reports of four more.

The screen showed five possible launches. Petrov later said the small number made him doubt that it was a real first strike. He also knew the satellite system had problems. Those clues gave him reasons to question the warning, but they did not prove it was false.

**[VISUAL]** Diagram of a satellite looking down at Earth. Show a sensor signal becoming a label on a console, then a person examining the label. A small group of five icons appears beside a much larger set of possible targets, without asserting a specific doctrine.  
**[ON-SCREEN TEXT]** `SENSOR READING ≠ CONFIRMED ATTACK`  
**[EDITOR NOTE]** Keep the diagram simple. The distinction is more important than technical detail.

### 02:15 — A tense moment

**[NARRATION]**  
The warning arrived during a period of deep distrust between Washington and Moscow. Just weeks earlier, the Soviet Union had shot down Korean Air Lines Flight 007 after the civilian passenger plane entered Soviet airspace. The incident had made relations even worse.

Petrov later described the atmosphere as tense. In that setting, an alarm could not be treated as a harmless computer glitch. If the warning was real, minutes mattered. If it was wrong, acting on it could help turn fear into a disaster.

The system was new and had limits. The warning came from satellites. Chatham House’s account says Petrov looked for confirmation from radar or telescopes, but none was available to confirm the satellite reading. He questioned why a real attack would appear as only a few launches.

Those were clues, not guarantees. Under pressure, he had fragments that pointed in different directions and little time to be certain.

**[VISUAL]** Timeline: Flight 007, 1 September 1983; false alarm, 26 September 1983. Show the two events as context, not as direct proof of Petrov’s thoughts. Then split the screen into “satellite warning” and “other evidence still needed.”  
**[SOUND]** Radio static briefly enters under “fragments,” then clears.

### 03:40 — The decision

**[NARRATION]**  
Petrov reported that he believed the warning was false. His job was to assess the signal and report his view to his superiors. Other officers and Soviet leaders would have taken part in any further decision.

Chatham House describes his report as helping stop the warning from moving further up the chain as a confirmed attack. It also notes disagreement about exactly how far the alert had already spread. His judgment mattered, but he was one person in a larger command system, not the sole person with control over a launch.

**[VISUAL]** The console warning moves through a chain of desks and phones. At Petrov’s station, the label changes from `LAUNCH DETECTED` to `ASSESSMENT: FALSE ALARM`. Keep the chain visible; do not depict him controlling weapons directly.  
**[EDITOR NOTE]** Pause briefly after “His judgment mattered.”

### 04:35 — What caused the warning?

**[NARRATION]**  
Later investigation traced the false warning to sunlight reflected from clouds. From the satellite’s angle, the flash looked like the bright infrared signal the system expected from a missile launch. In plain terms, a patch of reflected sunlight fooled the sensor.

This explanation came after the event. Petrov did not have the final answer while the warning lights were on. He had to judge the evidence he had, not the explanation investigators would find later.

That difference matters whenever we tell a story about a good decision. If we use facts discovered afterward as if the person knew them at the time, we make the choice look easier than it was.

**[VISUAL]** A simple geometry animation: satellite, sun, curved cloud tops. A reflected patch reaches the sensor and triggers the warning label. Use a caption: `Later investigation found sunlight reflected from clouds had confused the system.`  
**[SOUND]** The warning tone resolves into ordinary room hum.

### 05:40 — What we can say

**[NARRATION]**  
The false alarm exposed a weakness in a system meant to help leaders decide what was happening. The National Park Service credits Petrov’s judgment with preventing an erroneous retaliatory attack. Chatham House describes how his report slowed the warning’s path through the command system, while noting that parts of the sequence remain disputed.

We cannot know what would have happened if Petrov had reported the warning differently. We can say what did happen: he treated the flashing screen as a warning to investigate, reported his doubt, and was later proved right about the alarm. The satellite had mistaken reflected sunlight for a missile signal.

The report did not make Petrov a public hero at the time. The National Park Service says the incident exposed problems in the warning system and embarrassed his superiors. Petrov was denied promotions, reassigned, and later took early retirement. The event remained inside the secretive military world until the late 1990s.

The source records that sequence, though it cannot prove that this one night caused every later career decision. The contrast is still striking: the system depended on officers judging its warnings, yet the incident also showed how uncomfortable it could be for leaders when an officer revealed a flaw.

**[VISUAL]** Return to the five lights. One by one they turn off, leaving the reflection diagram in the dark. Then show the same control room as part of a larger chain of people and systems.  
**[SOUND]** No triumphant music. Let the final line sit over a quiet hum.

### 07:35 — Closing image

**[NARRATION]**  
The screen was wrong. Petrov did not know why. He had to make a judgment anyway.

Later, investigators found the reflected sunlight that had fooled the satellite. At the time, Petrov had only the warning, the missing confirmation, and his doubts. The space between those facts was where his decision happened.

**[VISUAL]** A satellite passes over a cloud bank. The reflection fades as the camera pulls back to show Earth at night. Fade to black.  
**[SOUND]** Hum ends; one second of silence.
