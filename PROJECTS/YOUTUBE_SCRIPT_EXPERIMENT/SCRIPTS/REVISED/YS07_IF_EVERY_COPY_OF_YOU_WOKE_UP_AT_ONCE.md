**Format:** Single narrator, philosophical thought experiment. **Audience:** General audience; explain each idea in everyday language. **Target:** 8–12 minutes. **Runtime:** `SCRIPT_ESTIMATE`; not recording- or animatic-measured. **Narration count:** 941 (deterministic count from `[NARRATION]` blocks). **Estimated runtime:** About 9:41–10:11 at 115 words per minute plus 90–120 seconds of planned visual holds. **Primary title:** If Two Copies of You Woke Up at Once. **Alternates:** Which One Is the Real You?; If Your Mind Was Copied Twice, Who Would You Be?; The Copy Problem That Breaks the Idea of You. **Thumbnail:** One silhouette facing two identical lit doorways; text `WHICH ONE IS YOU?`. **Promise:** Test what we mean by you when two people claim the same past.

## Alternative hooks

**A. Selected:** You wake up in a room with two people who look exactly like you. Both know your name. Both remember your childhood. Both insist they are the person who went to sleep. Which one would you call yourself?

**B. Alternate:** If someone made a perfect copy of your memories, would you wake up as that person—or would the copy simply wake up believing it was you?

Hook A gives the viewer an immediate choice and makes the hypothetical clear. Hook B states the puzzle more directly but has less narrative tension.

## Script

### 00:00 — Two people say they are you

**[NARRATION]**
You enter a fictional scanner. It makes two people with your memories. Now picture opening your eyes as one of them. The other has just opened their eyes too. Both know your name. Both remember your childhood. Which one, if either, would you call yourself?

This is not a prediction about technology. It is a thought experiment: a made-up story used to test an idea. The machine has copied a person so well that there are now two people with the same memories. The strange question is not which one looks real. It is whether either one is still you.

**[VISUAL]** One sleeping silhouette appears, then splits into two matching figures under separate lights. A card reads `THOUGHT EXPERIMENT — NOT REAL TECHNOLOGY`.
**[SOUND]** Soft hum that stops before the question.

### 01:10 — What makes you the same person?

**[NARRATION]**
We use “same” in more than one way. Two shirts can look the same without being one shirt. And every morning, you wake up after hours when you had no thoughts you can remember. You still assume the person who opened their eyes is the person who went to sleep, because one life continued through the night.

Philosophers call the deeper question personal identity: what makes someone now the same person as someone in the past? When you look at a childhood photo and say, “That was me,” you mean more than “that child looks like me.” You mean the child and the adult looking at the photo are one continuing person.

One answer points to the body. You are the same living human because your body continued through time. Another answer points to the mind: memories, habits, hopes, and ways of thinking. If those continue, perhaps you continue too.

In ordinary life, body and mind usually travel together. The puzzle begins when the story pulls them apart.

**[VISUAL]** A childhood photo connects to an adult silhouette with a continuous line. Then show two simple labels: `BODY` and `MEMORIES + HABITS`, both linked to one person.

### 02:40 — The first copy seems easy

**[NARRATION]**
Let’s start with only one copy. You enter a fictional scanner. It records every detail needed to build a person somewhere else. A machine makes a new body with your memories and personality. You open your eyes in another room, remember entering the scanner, and feel certain you are you.

Many people’s first reaction is, “That would be me.” The new person remembers the same friends, the same awkward school day, and the same promise made last week. From the inside, the story feels unbroken.

But another answer is possible. The original body may have stopped, while a new person woke up with copied memories. That person is not lying. The memories really feel like theirs. The question is whether feeling continuous proves that the same person continued.

There is no test in this story that settles the matter. That is the point. The machine gives us a case where our usual clues no longer point in the same direction.

**[VISUAL]** Show a scanner as a simple outline, with a memory map copied to a second figure. Keep the original figure’s fate unclear; do not show a body disappearing as if the story proves that death occurs.

### 04:20 — Now the machine makes two

**[NARRATION]**
Now change one thing. The scanner makes two copies, not one. They wake up in separate rooms. Both remember entering the scanner. Both have your habits. Both know what you were about to say.

If memories are what make you continue, both seem connected to you. But they cannot both be the one person who entered the machine. They are now two people. They can choose different breakfasts, make different friends, and disagree about what to do next.

The problem is called branching. One past appears to lead to two future people. If each copy is you, then you are two people at once. If only one is you, what makes that copy special? The machine treated them exactly alike.

Maybe the original person continues in neither copy. Maybe both are new people with a powerful connection to the original. Maybe our idea of identity cannot answer every question we want to ask.

**[VISUAL]** One path divides into two equal paths. The copies begin making different choices: one leaves, one stays. Caption: `Same starting memories. Two separate lives.`
**[EDITOR NOTE]** Do not present branching as an established scientific event. This is the philosophical puzzle.

### 06:20 — The answer may not be one label

**[NARRATION]**
Philosophers disagree about which part matters most. Some say the same living body is essential. Others say the right kind of mental connection matters more. Some reject the idea that one simple test can settle every case.

One influential philosopher, Derek Parfit, used copying and branching stories to ask whether being exactly the same person is all we care about. Before entering the scanner, you promised your sister you would call tomorrow. Both copies remember the promise. Both can call her. Even if neither is literally you, you might still care very much about what happens to each one.

That shifts the question. We may ask “Is it me?” when we also mean “Will my memories, plans, and relationships continue?” Those are not identical questions. A copy could continue some parts of your life without solving whether you survived.

This does not prove that identity is unimportant. It shows that several things we value—memory, relationships, responsibility, and being the same person—can come apart in the story.

**[VISUAL]** A set of cards—`same body`, `shared memories`, `relationships`, `responsibility`—separate and recombine. Put a small label `PHILOSOPHERS DISAGREE` on screen.

### 08:20 — Which one leaves?

**[NARRATION]**
Return to the room. Two people stand before you. Both remember your life. Both are ready to continue it. The thought experiment cannot point to one and prove, “That is the real you.” It has removed the clue we normally rely on: one continuous life, in one body, with one stream of memories.

You might still choose the copy who is kind, or the one who keeps a promise. But those choices tell us what matters about a person’s future, not which copy is secretly the original.

One possible answer is that the copies are two new people who inherited the same past. They are not interchangeable once their separate lives begin. They are each responsible for what they do next. This is one way to read the thought experiment, not a settled answer accepted by every philosopher.

So if a perfect copy woke up tomorrow, would it be you? Philosophy does not give us a settled answer. It gives us a sharper question: when you say you want to survive, do you mean that you want to remain the same person, or that the things you care about should continue?

**[VISUAL]** Return to the two doorways. The figures choose different doors; leave both in frame. End on the question, not on a “correct” answer.
**[SOUND]** Two soft door clicks; no dramatic sting.
