## Script

### 00:00 — The bridge moves

**[NARRATION]**
The bridge was not simply bouncing. One morning, its roadway began twisting so hard that one side rose while the other fell. The wind was not a hurricane. By late morning, sections of the bridge were breaking apart and falling into the water.

This was the Tacoma Narrows Bridge in Washington, nicknamed Galloping Gertie. It had been open only a few months. People had already seen it rise and fall in the wind. But on November 7, 1940, its motion changed. Why did that change matter more than the bouncing everyone knew?

**[VISUAL]** Draw a simple bridge across the narrows. First animate a gentle vertical wave. Then pause and rotate one side of the deck downward while the other rises. Label the motion `BOUNCE` then `TWIST`.
**[SOUND]** Low wind, cable hum; avoid disaster-film booms.

### 01:05 — A bridge that already moved

**[NARRATION]**
The new bridge was long, narrow, and light. That made it look elegant and helped keep construction costs down. But a bridge is not a solid bar. Its deck, cables, and towers flex. Wind can push on a structure and make it move.

From the start, drivers and workers felt Galloping Gertie rise and fall. The movement gave the bridge its nickname. Engineers tried to reduce the bounce. They added devices meant to act like shock absorbers, and later installed cables to hold parts of the bridge down. The fixes did not stop the center span’s motion.

Engineers knew the movement was unusual. A professor at the University of Washington was studying it with models in a wind tunnel. But knowing that something moves is not the same as knowing how it will fail.

**[VISUAL]** Simple side view: cars crossing as the deck rises and falls. Show shock absorbers at the towers and temporary tie-down cables. Use a scale model in a wind tunnel.

### 02:30 — The morning the motion changed

**[NARRATION]**
On the morning of November 7, steady winds pushed against the bridge’s solid sides. At first, the familiar waves continued. Cars crossed. Engineers watched. A photographer filmed the movement.

At about 10:03, the middle of the roadway began twisting. WSDOT’s timeline also records the first concrete falling at roughly this time. The change was easy to see: instead of the whole deck rising together, one side lifted as the other dropped. The bridge became a moving hinge.

One driver, Leonard Coatsworth, escaped from his car. His daughter’s dog, Tubby, was still inside. Engineers and photographers tried to reach the vehicle, but the deck was rolling under them. The bridge was no longer a road. It was a moving surface with no safe place to stand.

The damage continued in stages. Around 10:30, a large piece of concrete fell. Around 11:02, the center span began to break apart; by about 11:10, it was in Puget Sound. Near the end, professor F. B. Farquharson tried to reach Tubby but turned back after the frightened dog bit his hand. No person died. Tubby was lost with the car.

**[VISUAL]** Use a clock and a restrained overhead map to show the morning’s progression. Reconstruct the car and dog as simple shapes; do not use a distressing close-up. Show the roadway’s twist separately from the collapse.
**[SOUND]** Wind grows, then drops to near-silence as the center span falls.

### 04:30 — Why twisting is different

**[NARRATION]**
So what changed? It is tempting to say the wind simply found the bridge’s natural rhythm and made the bouncing bigger. That is the explanation many people remember. But the collapse was more complicated than a gust matching a swing.

The bridge’s deck was a solid plate, not an open frame. Wind hit its side. When the deck tilted a little, it changed the path of the air around it. That new airflow pushed on the deck in a way that could add more twist. The extra twist then changed the airflow again.

Think of pushing a playground swing at the right moment. Each push adds energy. Here, the bridge’s own movement helped change how the wind pushed it. Engineers call the main explanation torsional flutter. “Torsional” means twisting. “Flutter” means a motion that can feed itself and grow.

The wind supplied the energy, but the bridge’s shape and movement helped control where that energy went. Once the twist became strong, it could keep increasing even though the wind was not suddenly getting much stronger.

### 06:30 — The part engineers still debate

**[NARRATION]**
There is a useful limit to this explanation. Engineers still disagree about some details of exactly how the final motion began. The Washington State Department of Transportation describes torsional flutter as the main explanation, while also noting that the exact sequence has not reached complete agreement.

That does not mean the cause is a total mystery. The bridge was unusually flexible in twisting. Its solid sides interacted with the wind. The roadway shifted into a twisting motion, and the movement grew until the structure failed. The well-supported picture is a flexible deck interacting with wind and growing twist. The exact trigger and sequence are still debated.

The collapse also challenged a blind spot. Many engineers had focused on how bridges carried weight and resisted traffic. Wind was often treated as a smaller problem. Galloping Gertie showed that air could shape the movement of a bridge, not just shove against it.

**[VISUAL]** Two cards: `WELL SUPPORTED: flexible deck + wind + growing twist` and `DETAILS STILL STUDIED: exact trigger and sequence`. Show an engineer’s notebook, not a confident single “cause” stamp.

### 08:00 — What the bridge left behind

**[NARRATION]**
The lesson did not mean every bridge needed to be heavy and rigid. It meant designers had to test how air and structure move together. Wind-tunnel models and later engineering tools helped designers look for twisting as well as bending and bouncing.

The famous film of Galloping Gertie still looks unreal. But the strangest part is not that a bridge moved in the wind. Bridges are built to flex. The strange part is that a small change in motion changed the wind’s effect, until the bridge and the air kept feeding the same twist.

The famous film still shows a bridge seeming to dance. But the motion was not harmless. Before another bridge crossed the water, engineers had to understand what the wind could make its deck do.
