# Series Format — Shared-Universe Thematic Anthology

## Status

Draft format profile for research and pilot validation. It is not yet an active project selector or runtime instruction.

## Format promise

Each episode offers a meaningful story experience of its own while contributing to a larger fictional universe. Viewers should be able to enter through an individual episode without requiring complete knowledge of every previous instalment.

## Episode unit

An episode may introduce:
- A new mystery, case, folklore event or local conflict.
- A protagonist or recurring investigator.
- A location, community, artefact or supernatural rule.
- A complete episode-level dramatic question and outcome.

An episode should resolve its promised central story question unless the project intentionally declares a multi-part story.

## Shared-universe continuity

The series bible may track:
- Recurring characters and relationships.
- Locations and institutions.
- Established supernatural rules.
- Historical events and chronology.
- Recurring objects, symbols and motifs.
- Consequences of earlier cases.
- Open long-term questions.

Separate established canon, proposed additions, disputed facts and unresolved questions.

## Long-term story arcs

A larger arc may develop through small clues, recurring antagonists, connected cases or changes in the world. Such an arc is optional and should not make every episode feel like an advertisement for the next one.

A recurring mystery should reward attentive viewers without making individual cases incomprehensible.

## Viewer entry points

For each episode, record:
- What a first-time viewer needs to know.
- What returning viewers may recognise.
- Whether prior episodes are required.
- Any continuity reference that should be explained visually or briefly.

## Production implications

Identify recurring assets that can be reused:
- Main characters and character rigs.
- Locations and set modules.
- Props and artefacts.
- Visual motifs.
- Music and sound motifs.

Reuse must not prevent a story from introducing new visual material when it has narrative value.

## Ending

An episode ending may:
- Resolve the individual case.
- Show its emotional or practical consequence.
- Introduce a small shared-universe development.
- Leave an optional question for later episodes.

Avoid withholding the core resolution solely to force a next-episode click.

## Common failure modes

- Making every episode dependent on previous episodes.
- Confusing mystery with missing explanation.
- Using unexplained continuity as a substitute for a satisfying ending.
- Introducing lore that contradicts established rules.
- Repeating the same case structure without meaningful variation.
- Expanding the universe faster than production can maintain continuity.

## Review checklist

- [ ] The episode has a clear central story question.
- [ ] A first-time viewer can follow the episode.
- [ ] Its main conflict receives an appropriate resolution.
- [ ] Continuity references are accurate.
- [ ] New canon is labelled as proposed until approved.
- [ ] Recurring assets are identified without constraining the story unnecessarily.
- [ ] Any larger arc adds value without replacing the episode's own payoff.

## Interaction with genre and channel style

Genre profiles govern narrative conventions and genre-specific expectations. This format profile governs episode relationships and continuity. The channel style guide governs presentation, audience promise, packaging and publishing identity. None may silently overwrite the others.
